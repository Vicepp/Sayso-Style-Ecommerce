Why we stopped buying new wool

Three seasons ago we switched the entire knitwear line to deadstock and reclaimed fibre. It cost us range, lead time and a supplier relationship.

The decision started as an accounting question and turned into a design one. We had been buying virgin merino by the cone, forecasting a year out, and writing off whatever the season did not sell. The write-offs were not small. They were also, quietly, the single largest environmental cost in the business — more than shipping, more than the studio, more than everything else combined.

So we stopped. Not gradually, and not as a marketing position. We simply placed no new virgin wool orders for autumn, and set ourselves the problem of building a range from what already existed.

What we found

There is an enormous amount of finished yarn sitting in European warehouses. Mills over-run. Brands cancel. A colour gets approved and then rejected six weeks later, and forty cones of a perfectly good lambswool go into storage indefinitely. None of it is waste in any technical sense. It is simply inventory that nobody has a use for.

Buying this way inverts the design process. You do not choose a colour and go find it. You find a colour and decide whether you can build something honest around it.

That inversion is the hard part. Our first deadstock season had four colourways instead of nine, and two of them were decided by what was available in sufficient quantity rather than by any considered palette. The oat we now use in almost everything was, originally, an accident.

What it cost

  • Range shrank by roughly forty per cent in the first season
  • Lead times became unpredictable — you buy when stock appears, not when you need it
  • We lost a mill relationship of six years, which still stings
  • Repeat colours became impossible to guarantee season to season

That last one matters more than it sounds. A customer who loved the charcoal crew in year one cannot reliably buy the same charcoal in year three. We have had to be honest about that on every product page, and some people find it frustrating. Fair enough.

What it saved

Materially: about sixty per cent of the carbon attributable to our fibre, and a write-off line that has almost disappeared, because we now buy in quantities we can actually sell. Less measurably, it made the range better. Fewer options, chosen more carefully, worn longer.

We are not claiming this is the answer for everyone. A brand at ten thousand units cannot source this way; the volumes are not there. At our size it works, and at our size the alternative was indefensible.

If you want the full fibre breakdown for any piece, it is on the product page, and if it is not clear enough, write to us and we will send you the supplier detail.
